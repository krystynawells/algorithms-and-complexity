Inside Machines We Don’t Understand — Interactive Reading

An interactive Week 4 reading based on Harari’s “Taking Responsibility”. Use the tools below to explore, reflect, and test your understanding.

We live in an age of effortless connection. With a phone in hand, you can reach anyone in your circle, or even a stranger halfway across the globe. This web of communication, once unimaginable, has become an everyday part of life. Harari’s “Taking Responsibility” prompts a deeper question: to what level do we actually understand or even comprehend this technology today?

Harari frames responsibility as the duty to understand the systems that structure society — not to master every technical detail, but to recognise their social impact.

In Taking Responsibility, Harari argues that technology’s complexity has outpaced our capacity to understand it. We depend on networks, algorithms and infrastructures that operate outside of our awareness. This growing gap between use and understanding isn’t just a technical issue; it’s a social and moral one. To live responsibly in a digital world means learning to see the hidden structures that shape our choices.

Examples of "hidden structures" include recommendation systems, data pipelines, and opaque machine-learning models that adapt based on user data.

This lack of understanding has serious consequences. Harari illustrates how powerful corporations, such as Facebook, have influenced real-world events while denying responsibility for their impact. For example, he refers to how Facebook’s algorithms contributed to the spread of hate speech during the Rohingya crisis in Myanmar, where online misinformation and incitement led to real-world violence and persecution. Facebook's algorithms boosted content with little regard for the truth. Even Facebook (now Meta) has admitted that it doesn’t fully understand how its own algorithms work. Algorithms decide what we see, what we believe, and even how we feel, yet their logic remains hidden. This imbalance of knowledge creates a new kind of power: those who understand and control the systems can shape reality for those who don’t. In that sense, ignorance isn’t neutral; it becomes a tool of influence.

UN and investigative reports tied Facebook’s role in amplifying hate speech to the Rohingya crisis — an example of how platform design can have real-world harms.

Harari’s call to "take responsibility" ultimately challenges us to reclaim awareness in a world built on systems we can no longer see. Understanding technology isn’t about mastering every algorithm but recognising the influence it holds over our attention, our politics, and our sense of truth. When ignorance becomes the norm, power concentrates in the hands of the few who can manipulate what the rest of us cannot comprehend. To live ethically in the digital age, we must move beyond passive use toward active understanding questioning not just what our technologies do, but what they do to us.

Taking responsibility can include demanding transparency, supporting regulation, and building public digital literacy.
